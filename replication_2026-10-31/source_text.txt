We develop a framework for simulating professional expectations formation using large
language models (LLMs). Combining novel hand-collected data on Survey of Professional
Forecasters(SPF)participantcharacteristicswithreal-timemacroeconomicdataandlagged
SPF median forecasts, we prompt LLMs to generate quarterly forecastsfor 23 variables over
1999-2023. Theresultingsyntheticpanelapproximateskeypropertiesofthehumansurvey,
including forecast accuracy, median forecast levels, revision dynamics, and cross-sectional
dispersion. Ablation exercises show that performance is largely driven by information
conditioning: removing forecaster characteristics modestly worsens performance, whereas
removing real-time data and especially lagged SPF medians leads to substantial error in-
creases. Ourframeworkoffersascalablecomplementtotraditionalsurveys,enablingcoun-
terfactualanalysisunderalternativeinformationsets, retrospective“as-of”forecasting, and
rapid prototyping of survey designs.
Keywords: Generative Artificial Intelligence; Survey of Professional Forecasters; Expecta-
tions Formation; Simulated Economic Agents.

1 Introduction
Expectationsareacentralobjectinmonetaryeconomics. Theyshapepolicytransmission,disci-
pline models of information rigidity, and provide real-time measures of beliefs about inflation,
output, and interest rates. Recognizing this, economists and policymakers rely on surveys as
a measurement tool to capture expectations about future economic variables and conditions
(e.g., Rudebusch, 2002; Orphanides and Williams, 2002, 2007; Patton and Timmermann, 2010;
Manzan,2011,2021;Almåsetal.,2024;Caplin,2025). However,traditionalsurveysfaceinherent
limitations.
Surveys are costly to administer, constraining both sampling frequency and sample size.
They lack flexibility, e.g., it is difficult to revise existing questions or add new ones, and new
questions lack historical responses, limiting their value for time series analysis. Beyond that,
most macroeconomic surveys solicit numeric answers with little insight into the reasoning
behind them. Finally, surveys depend heavily on institutional infrastructure and funding, the
stability of which has come under stress in recent years. These limitations have prompted
calls for methodological innovation (Caplin, 2025). At the same time, a growing literature is
exploring whether LLMs can serve as useful models of human behavior and expectations, and
whether they can become a valuable new research tool in economics (e.g., see Jackson et al.,
2025). Although recent studies suggest that LLMs can reproduce aspects of human decision-
making and expectations, we still know relatively little about which dimensions of behavior
they capture well, where they systematically differ from humans, and for which applications
they are most useful. We contribute to this emerging literature by focusing on professional
macroeconomic forecasters, using the Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF) as a laboratory
for evaluating the promise and limitations of LLM-based synthetic agents, and show that they
can help mitigate some of these limitations.
We develop a framework that uses LLMs to simulate individual participants in the SPF.
Because LLMs are trained on large-scale text data, they can capture systematic patterns in how
peopledescribe,interpret,andjustifydecisions,whichcanbeleveragedtoapproximatehuman
reasoning in structured tasks (Suri et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2024). Recent work further shows
thatLLMscanreproducebehavioralpatternsofeconomicagentsinexperimentalcontexts(e.g.,
Horton, 2023; Hansen and Lee, 2025; Kazinnik, 2026; Zarifhonarvar, 2026). In our setting, this

judgment in point forecasts has increased” during “periods of economic volatility” such as
the global financial crisis and pandemic (European Central Bank, 2024). Our results suggest
thatAIforecasters,liketheirhumancounterparts,adapttheirreasoningapproachtoeconomic
conditions, relying more heavily on judgment and event-specific narratives during periods of
heightened uncertainty. While more work is needed to fully characterize and validate LLM
reasoning, this analysis suggests that our framework could be extended to simulate not only
forecasts but also the reasoning processes that professional forecasters employ.
6 Conclusion
Inthispaper,weintroduceanewframeworkformodelingexpertexpectations’formationusing
large language models. Rather than treating the LLM primarily as a forecasting tool, we use it
as a model of forecasters: an expectations operator that maps real-time data, lagged forecasts,
and forecaster characteristics into survey responses.

=== PDF PAGE 28 ===
Using this framework, we construct a synthetic panel of forecasters designed to mirror the
institutionalenvironmentoftheSurveyofProfessionalForecasters. Weshowthattheresulting
AI-generatedpanelreproducessalientfeaturesofthehumansurvey,includingmedianforecast
levels. Importantly, while aligned in many dimensions, AI and human forecasts differ in
systematicandinterpretablewaysthatreflectdifferencesininformationavailability,information
processing, and updating behavior.
Our ablation exercises clarify why this occurs. Performance improves notably when the
LLM is augmented with lagged human forecasts, real-time data, and forecaster characteris-
tics. Removingtheseinputssystematicallyworsensperformance,withthelargestdeterioration
occurring when past SPF medians are excluded. Lagged human forecasts are useful because
they capture things we cannot easily measure, like judgment, context, and institutional knowl-
edge. By using these forecasts, the model can better mimic how people form expectations.
We therefore view the framework not as a replacement for human survey participants, but as a
complementarymeasurementtoolthatenablescontrolledanalysisofhowexpectationsrespond
to alternative information environments.
A key advantage of LLM-based panels is their flexibility. Traditional surveys cannot retroac-
tivelymodifypanelcomposition,controlinformationavailability,orexperimentwithalternative
question designs. LLM-based panels enable all of these counterfactual analyses with appro-
priate context engineering. This, in turn, allows researchers to prototype new survey designs
at minimal cost and simulate counterfactual forecast distributions for past periods under cus-
tom information sets. More broadly, our results point to a substantive role for generative AI
in macroeconomics: not as a forecasting oracle, but as a laboratory for studying expectations
formation. Bycombininginstitutionalrealismwithflexiblecontextengineering,theframework
allows researchers to analyze expectations in a disciplined and transparent way. We see this
approach as a step toward integrating modern machine learning tools into the measurement
of expectations in a manner that complements economic theory and existing survey-based
methods.
Integration of human expertise with AI-based forecaster panels, analogous to a “man+ ma-
chine” approach in other domains, promises richer measurements of expectations and deeper
understanding of belief formation in macroeconomic environments. The human SPF provides

D Simulating the SPF With an AR(1) Model
This appendix shows forecast results generated by a simple AR(1) model on median human
forecasts. Specifically, letting¯ht denote the median human forecast of any variable in the SPF,
the AR(1) model takes the following form:
¯ht = α + ρ¯ht−1 + εt, (D.13)
where εt is independent and identically distributed according to a normal distribution with
meanzeroandaconstantvariance. Thecoefficientsareestimatedusinganexpandingwindow,
such that predictions for the survey at timet are based on information up until timet − 1.
Table D.15 reports mean absolute errors of AI-generated SPF forecasts and forecasts implied
by an AR(1) model on median human SPF forecasts, relative to the mean absolute errors
of human SPF forecasts. The statistical significance of the difference between AI and AR(1)
forecastsisevaluatedusingtheDieboldandMarianotest(DieboldandMariano(1995),Diebold
et al. (1999)). The results show that human forecasters do not rely solely on AR(1) models for
generating SPF forecasts, as the relative mean absolute error of AR(1) forecasts are generally
different from one. In addition, the results show that AI forecasts add value beyond the AR(1)
model,astheAIandAR(1)modelarestatisticallydifferentandtherelativemeanabsoluteerror
is lower for AI forecasts compared with AR(1) forecasts, almost everywhere.
