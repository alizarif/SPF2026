Thus, the results in Table 9 provide evidence against a strong form of look-ahead bias. If the
model were using future information, its forecasts should become unusually accurate around
unexpected events. We do not find such a pattern: errors are generally typical or larger than
usual. This evidence is not definitive, but it suggests that the model is not simply retrieving
realized outcomes around salient historical events. Similar results are obtained by averaging
across all 23 survey variables in Panel B of Table 9.
Finally, we evaluate forecast accuracy in an out-of-sample exercise on 2024 Q1:Q4 data,
which excludes the training data sample and therefore eliminates any concerns about temporal
leakage by construction. Figure 6 shows forecast errors for one-quarter ahead forecasts around
the training cut-off date for the four key variables. The figure indicates that there is no clear
structural break in the forecast error, or in how the error of AI-generated forecasts differ from
the human forecast error, around the cut-off date, suggesting that the performance of the model
is not driven by temporal leakage. We observe slightly larger deviations for the 3-month bill rate
and real GDP in the final quarter of 2024 (which is not around the cut-off), which may be due
to the model’s lack of access to real-time major developments—such as election-related news,
updates on monetary policy (the FOMC announced a cut in the federal funds rate in September
2024), and other current events. The findings in Figure 6 are supported by regressing absolute
AI forecast errors on a dummy variable that takes value one after the cut-off date (October 2023).
Table 10 should be interpreted as a formal break test around the model’s knowledge cut-off.
The post-cutoff coefficients are insignificant, indicating that AI forecast errors do not increase
discontinuously after October 2023, the model’s knowledge cutoff.
