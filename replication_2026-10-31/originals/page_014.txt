We start by collecting forecaster names and affiliations from the Philadelphia Fed acknowledg-
ments. Using this information, we explore publicly available sources (e.g., personal websites,
LinkedIn, etc.), and construct a unique dataset of individual forecaster characteristics. We cre-
ate detailed personas for each forecaster by gathering key background information, including
education, job titles, affiliated organizations, alma mater, degrees, and professional roles. When
available, we also include organization locations, countries of origin, and social media pres-
ence. For country of origin, we consider indicators like high school location, if available.11 We
describe these characteristics in Table 2.
Empirical research shows that personal backgrounds strongly influence people’s behavior and
beliefs. For example, individuals who experienced the Great Depression are often more cautious
with financial risk (Malmendier and Nagel, 2011), while those who experienced hyperinflation
may avoid risky assets (Fajardo and Dantas, 2018). Salle et al. (2026) and Malmendier and
Nagel (2016) also show that memories and personal experiences of inflation shape inflation
expectations. Geography and institutional affiliation could also impact forecasting approaches
(Batchelor, 2007; Hong and Kacperczyk, 2010).
We condition the model only on forecaster characteristics that plausibly map into systematic,
interpretable differences in information sets, incentives, and forecasting styles. In practice, the
most consequential fields are (i) affiliation/sector (e.g., academia, commercial bank, consulting,
government), which proxies for institutional objectives and exposure to particular data sources
and modeling traditions; (ii) job title/role, which captures the forecaster’s function (research,
markets, policy analysis, macro strategy) and therefore the likely salience of specific variables
and horizons; and (iii) education and training, which proxy for methodological priors and
comfort with structural versus reduced-form reasoning. These inputs are not meant to “iden-
tify” individuals or reproduce private information. Rather, they provide coarse but meaningful
variation that can generate heterogeneity in levels, disagreement, and updating, mirroring the
fact that SPF participants differ in institutional constraints, attention, and modeling approaches
even when observing similar public macro data.
