macroeconomic variables over a 24-year period, our setting provides a rich environment for
evaluating LLMs as behavioral models of professional forecasters rather than merely as fore-
casting algorithms. This distinction changes the object of validation. We do not evaluate the
LLM only by asking whether it minimizes forecast errors.
Instead, we ask whether it can
reproduce survey-relevant moments of professional expectations: median forecasts, disagree-
ment, revision behavior, and reasoning patterns. This moves the exercise from macroeconomic
forecasting to expectations measurement. Multiple papers have subsequently followed our ap-
proach and corroborate some of our results in other contexts, e.g., Wu et al. (2025) and Graham
et al. (2026).
Our paper is also related to Ghysels and Wright (2009), who use asset-price data, MIDAS re-
gressions, and Kalman filtering to infer higher-frequency measures of professional forecasters’
expectations. Their approach addresses the staleness of survey releases by predicting upcoming
SPF releases and estimating what forecasters would report if surveyed at a higher frequency.
Although our validation focuses on the quarterly SPF, our framework shares the broader ob-
jective of increasing the temporal resolution of expectations measurement. The key difference
is that we simulate the survey respondents themselves. This individual-level structure allows
us to generate not only synthetic aggregate forecasts, but also disagreement, reasoning, and
counterfactual responses under controlled information sets. This work also intersects with
recent efforts to use machine learning to replicate or complement expert judgment in financial
domains. Murray et al. (2024) use deep learning to mimic technical chartists. Cao et al. (2024)
develop an AI equity analyst that processes corporate disclosures, quantitative fundamentals,
and macro indicators; its price targets outperform those of most human analysts, and a com-
bined man plus machine forecast performs best. This work differs from this line of literature in
two ways: our domain is macroeconomic surveys rather than securities analysis, and we treat
the LLM as an object of study, rather than as a forecasting tool.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 outlines our framework for under-
standing AI and human expectation formation. Section 3 details the structure and institutional
context of the SPF. We describe how we extract information on the participants in the SPF
panel and outline our methodology for generating synthetic forecasts using LLMs in Section 4.
Section 5 describes the results, and Section 6 concludes.
