Table 12 reports the prevalence of each reasoning type across normal and crisis periods,
showing both the detailed categories and the ECB-style aggregation. During normal periods,
model-based reasoning dominates (99.6%), with judgment-based reasoning at 63.3%. However,
during crisis periods, AI forecasters shift markedly toward judgment-based reasoning. The most
dramatic shift occurs in event-driven reasoning, which rises from 19.7% in normal periods to
82.4% during the financial crisis and 77.0% during COVID-19. Mechanical reasoning drops
substantially (from 98.6% to 72.1–84.7%), while the inflation shock period shows a distinct
pattern: policy-based reasoning surges to 87.3% as forecasters emphasize Federal Reserve
tightening.
The ECB-style aggregation reveals the broader pattern. During crises, judgment-based rea-
soning rises sharply: from 63.3% in normal periods to 87.6% during the financial crisis, 79.9%
during COVID-19, and 87.7% during the inflation shock. Model-based reasoning declines most
dramatically during COVID-19 (to 72.5%), likely reflecting the unprecedented nature of the
pandemic shock that rendered historical patterns less informative. All differences between
crisis and normal periods are statistically significant at conventional levels (p < 0.001).
These patterns are qualitatively consistent with evidence from special surveys of human
professional forecasters. The ECB’s Survey of Professional Forecasters finds that “the role of
judgment in point forecasts has increased” during “periods of economic volatility” such as
the global financial crisis and pandemic (European Central Bank, 2024). Our results suggest
that AI forecasters, like their human counterparts, adapt their reasoning approach to economic
conditions, relying more heavily on judgment and event-specific narratives during periods of
heightened uncertainty. While more work is needed to fully characterize and validate LLM
reasoning, this analysis suggests that our framework could be extended to simulate not only
forecasts but also the reasoning processes that professional forecasters employ.

Conclusion
In this paper, we introduce a new framework for modeling expert expectations’ formation using
large language models. Rather than treating the LLM primarily as a forecasting tool, we use it
as a model of forecasters: an expectations operator that maps real-time data, lagged forecasts,
and forecaster characteristics into survey responses.
