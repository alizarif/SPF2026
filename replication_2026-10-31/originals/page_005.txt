This heterogeneity is often attributed to differences in information sets, interpretation of
information, attention, behavioral biases, forecasting methods, and subjective models of the
economy. We capture some of these features by explicitly mapping observed forecaster char-
acteristics into distinct LLM-based personas, and by studying how these personas translate a
common real-time information set into different survey responses. And, because our AI fore-
casters provide narrative justifications for every prediction, we shed light on the “black box”
of expectations formation. We show that the simulated forecasters exhibit substantial hetero-
geneity in their reasoning, drawing on policy considerations, mechanical updating rules, recent
events, and theoretical models. These findings are broadly consistent with evidence from sur-
veys of professional forecasters, which show that forecasters combine model-based projections
with judgment, with judgment playing a more prominent role during periods of heightened
economic uncertainty (European Central Bank, 2024). While not a direct validation exercise,
this consistency lends credibility to our interpretation of the synthetic panel as an informative
model for studying professional expectations formation.
An important and commonly cited concern with models like LLMs is temporal data contami-
nation, which occurs when the model has been trained on information that was published after
the forecast date, potentially biasing its predictions (e.g., Lopez-Lira et al., 2025; He et al., 2025).
This issue is relevant for us because we interpret the LLM as approximating an SPF forecaster
who only has access to information available at the time of the survey. If the model heavily
relies on ex post realizations, its forecasts risk reflecting knowledge of the future rather than
expectations formed in real time. While individual-level SPF forecasts are unlikely to appear
in training data, the model may have access to realized macroeconomic outcomes that the SPF
aims to predict.
