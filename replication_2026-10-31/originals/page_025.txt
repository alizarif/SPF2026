As for the consensus tracking behavior, human forecasters show statistically significant
but economically modest convergence toward the SPF median: coefficients range from −0.30
(TBILL) to −0.87 (UNEMP), but R2 values are uniformly low (0.004–0.14). Distance from con-
sensus explains little of the variation in human forecast revisions, suggesting that professional
forecasters maintain independent views rather than systematically anchoring on the panel me-
dian. AI forecasters exhibit considerably stronger consensus tracking. For RGDP, the coefficient
is −1.02 with R2 = 0.78: forecasters who deviate from the SPF median by $X billion revise their
forecast by approximately $X billion toward consensus. TBILL shows even stronger anchoring
(−1.14, R2 = 0.72). CPI displays moderate consensus tracking (−0.69, R2 = 0.55), while un-
employment shows the weakest convergence (−0.51, R2 = 0.09). Thus, the AI panel captures
some qualitative features of updating, but it is more consensus-anchored than the human SPF.
These results reveal a fundamental difference in how human and AI forecasters form expec-
tations. Human forecasters behave as independent information processors: they place more
weight on correcting past errors (learning from mistakes) but place comparatively little weight
on the consensus view (maintaining idiosyncratic beliefs). AI forecasters exhibit the opposite
pattern: they update more gradually in response to past errors but strongly anchor on the
professional consensus, particularly for output and interest rate forecasts.
This difference has important implications for interpreting our framework. The strong con-
sensus anchoring by AI forecasters validates our ablation results in Section 5.2: removing past
SPF forecasts significantly increases forecast errors precisely because AI forecasters treat the
consensus as a highly informative summary of latent information used by human forecasters.
At the same time, this anchoring behavior also means that AI forecasts may inherit biases
present in the SPF consensus, and may be slower to deviate from consensus during periods
of structural change when independent judgment becomes especially valuable. The variable-
specific patterns (stronger anchoring for RGDP and TBILL than for CPI and unemployment)
suggest that AI forecasters weight the consensus signal differently depending on the variable,
potentially reflecting differences in how these indicators are discussed in training data. We view
