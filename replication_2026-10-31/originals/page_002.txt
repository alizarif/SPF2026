Introduction
Expectations are a central object in monetary economics. They shape policy transmission, disci-
pline models of information rigidity, and provide real-time measures of beliefs about inflation,
output, and interest rates. Recognizing this, economists and policymakers rely on surveys as
a measurement tool to capture expectations about future economic variables and conditions
(e.g., Rudebusch, 2002; Orphanides and Williams, 2002, 2007; Patton and Timmermann, 2010;
Manzan, 2011, 2021; Almås et al., 2024; Caplin, 2025). However, traditional surveys face inherent
limitations.
Surveys are costly to administer, constraining both sampling frequency and sample size.
They lack flexibility, e.g., it is difficult to revise existing questions or add new ones, and new
questions lack historical responses, limiting their value for time series analysis. Beyond that,
most macroeconomic surveys solicit numeric answers with little insight into the reasoning
behind them. Finally, surveys depend heavily on institutional infrastructure and funding, the
stability of which has come under stress in recent years. These limitations have prompted
calls for methodological innovation (Caplin, 2025). At the same time, a growing literature is
exploring whether LLMs can serve as useful models of human behavior and expectations, and
whether they can become a valuable new research tool in economics (e.g., see Jackson et al.,
2025). Although recent studies suggest that LLMs can reproduce aspects of human decision-
making and expectations, we still know relatively little about which dimensions of behavior
they capture well, where they systematically differ from humans, and for which applications
they are most useful. We contribute to this emerging literature by focusing on professional
macroeconomic forecasters, using the Survey of Professional Forecasters (SPF) as a laboratory
for evaluating the promise and limitations of LLM-based synthetic agents, and show that they
can help mitigate some of these limitations.
We develop a framework that uses LLMs to simulate individual participants in the SPF.

people describe, interpret, and justify decisions, which can be leveraged to approximate human
reasoning in structured tasks (Suri et al., 2024; Niu et al., 2024). Recent work further shows
that LLMs can reproduce behavioral patterns of economic agents in experimental contexts (e.g.,
