Figure 5 shows density plots of individual expectations across forecasters for four key
episodes: 1999Q1 (earliest observation), 2008Q3 (Great Financial Crisis), 2020Q2 (COVID-19
pandemic), and 2023Q1 (latest survey with four-quarter-ahead realizations). Each plot is cen-
tered around realized values. In some episodes, the distributions of human and AI expectations
align almost perfectly (see, e.g., the one-quarter ahead expectations for the unemployment rate
and real GDP in 2008Q3). In addition, the dispersion of real GDP expectations in 2020Q2 is un-
usually high for both AI and human forecasters. In other episodes, there are notable differences
(e.g., one-quarter-ahead expectations for real GDP and the unemployment rate in 1999Q1). The
extent to which human and AI forecast distributions align is thus highly dependent on the vari-
able, forecast horizon, and quarter. We also do not see a simple pattern in which humans and
AI are systematically more or less aligned during high-uncertainty episodes such as 2008Q3
and 2020Q2. However, cross-sectional forecast dispersion exhibits a common pattern across
both panels: disagreement rises with the forecast horizon, reflecting greater uncertainty about
more distant outcomes. Table 7 reports cross-sectional standard deviations at short (h+1) and
long (h+4) horizons. As in the SPF, disagreement in our AI panel is greater at longer horizons
for real variables. For unemployment, the ratio of long-horizon to short-horizon dispersion is
1.53 in the LLM panel versus 2.29 in the SPF (both p < 0.001). For real GDP, the corresponding
ratios are 1.40 and 2.49. However, neither panel exhibits this pattern for CPI inflation: the SPF
actually shows less dispersion at longer horizons (σh+4/σh+1 = 0.72, p < 0.001), while the LLM
panel shows no significant difference. This asymmetry, increasing disagreement with horizon
for real variables but not for inflation, likely reflects anchored long-run inflation expectations,
and the AI forecasters replicate this qualitative feature of human forecast disagreement.
Taken together, these results are consistent with our interpretation of the LLM as an imperfect
but informative model of expectations formation.
