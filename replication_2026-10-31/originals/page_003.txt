To build our simulation framework, we combine three inputs: (1) a novel dataset of hand-
collected SPF participant characteristics (e.g., education, affiliation, experience), (2) real-time
macroeconomic data available at each survey date, and (3) lagged median SPF forecasts. Us-
ing these inputs, we prompt an LLM to generate individual forecasts for 23 macroeconomic
variables across 100 quarterly surveys from 1999Q1 to 2023Q4, creating a synthetic panel of
professional forecasters. We use this setting to study three questions about professional ex-
pectations formation. First, to what extent can professional expectations be reproduced from
common real-time information and lagged consensus forecasts? Second, can synthetic fore-
casters reproduce economically relevant features of survey expectations, such as disagreement,
horizon effects, and forecast revisions? Third, where do synthetic forecasters differ from human
professionals, and what do these differences reveal about the role of independent judgment in
expectations formation?
To address these questions, we validate this synthetic panel against the human SPF along
several dimensions. The two panels share important similarities. Their forecasts are broadly
comparable. Both undershoot and overshoot the same variables in the same survey quarters
(e.g., CPI inflation is highly overshot in 2008:Q3 and undershot in 2020:Q2). The two panels
also show similar patterns of forecast dispersion: disagreement among forecasters is greater
at longer horizons than at shorter ones. Despite broad similarities, AI-generated and human
forecasts differ in statistically and economically meaningful ways. AI forecasters exhibit lower
disagreement, update their forecasts more strongly toward consensus, and place less weight on
correcting past forecast errors than human professionals. These findings suggest that synthetic
forecasters reproduce the common component of professional expectations more readily than
the independent judgment embodied in the SPF. These differences likely reflect the distinct
information environments, decision processes, and constraints faced by human and AI fore-
casters. Human forecasters face attention costs, reputation concerns, strategic considerations,
and heterogeneous judgment, while AI agents generate forecasts under standardized prompts
and controlled information sets. Given these differences and the limited input data we provide,
exact alignment at the level of point forecasts should not be expected. Taken together, our
